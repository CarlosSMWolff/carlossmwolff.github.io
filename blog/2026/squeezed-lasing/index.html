Squeezed lasing in atomic cavity QED

The squeezed laser is a unique source that combines coherence and sub-vacuum fluctuations in a single device. We have just proposed the first optical implementation of this device that does not require squeezing to be injected from an external environment, using rubidium atoms trapped inside a cavity. Here is an interactive explanation of the main concepts of the paper, which I prepared with heavy use of Claude.

Part 1

Light is a noisy wave

Light is an electromagnetic wave. As such, it suffers from the quantum noise enforced by Heisenberg's uncertainty principle into any quantum-mechanical object. Even vacuum (a state with absolutely no photons) features a minimum, unavoidable amount of noise, meaning the electromagnetic field fluctuates, even in its quietest state, no matter what we do.

Squeezing is our only way to tame this noise and trick it to decrease along one quadrature of the field, at the price of getting more noise in another.

Let's go deeper into these ideas.

A wave is the shadow of a rotating point

Take the light in one mode of a cavity, at a single frequency \(\omega\). It is described by two oscillating quantities, the electric field \(E(t)\) and the magnetic field \(B(t)\), a quarter of a period out of step: when one peaks, the other vanishes. Picture them as the two shadows of a single point going round a circle at frequency \(\omega\). The point's horizontal position, recorded over time below the circle, is \(E(t)\); its vertical position, recorded to the right, is \(B(t)\). That is why the whole field is a point in a plane: it takes two numbers to describe it.

The radius of the circle is the amplitude of the wave. Where the point sits at a given instant sets its phase. From now on we keep only the electric field, the shadow that a detector usually records.

Quantum noise: the point becomes a cloud

Quantum mechanics does not allow a sharp point. Measure the same light many times and each run lands somewhere slightly different. Every dot is one measurement, and together the dots fill a fuzzy cloud that rotates with the point.

The shadow of the cloud is a noisy wave: a band of uncertainty around the oscillation, equally thick at all times.

Stop the rotation

The rotation itself is the same for all light at frequency \(\omega\) and carries no information. So we watch from a frame that turns along with the light, and the cloud stops.

The centre of the cloud now has fixed coordinates, the field quadratures \(X\) and \(P\). Together they fix both the electric and the magnetic field, with \(E(t)=X\cos\omega t+P\sin\omega t\). We choose the frame so the cloud comes to rest on the \(X\) axis: \(X\) is then the amplitude and \(P=0\). This still plane is phase space. The cloud's width is the vacuum noise, \(\Delta X=\Delta P=1\). With no light at all, the same cloud sits at the origin.

Squeezing: reshaping the cloud

Heisenberg's principle limits only the product, \(\Delta X\,\Delta P\ge 1\). A squeezed state uses that freedom. The cloud is pressed to \(\Delta X=e^{-r}\), below the vacuum width, and stretched to \(\Delta P=e^{r}\). The number \(r\) is the squeezing parameter.

Let the rotation run again, and see what the shadow tells us about the time evolution. The wave is quieter than vacuum at the peaks, and noisier than vacuum at the zeros. A measurement that reads only \(X\) sees less noise than any classical light allows! This is useful, very useful, for applications like metrology, where the noise limits the precision of our measurements (think of detecting gravitational waves).

The squeezed laser

An ordinary laser emits a round cloud far from the origin of phase space. Its phase drifts slowly, so over long times the cloud smears around a ring. A squeezed laser would emit a squeezed cloud instead. It is bright like a laser and keeps a laser's long coherence time, but it also has the narrow noise in \(X\). Its phase drifts too, while the squeezed axis stays where it is.

Sketch in phase space. The cloud moves away from the vacuum (dashed circle) as the laser turns on; the faint copies show its phase drifting over long times.

We know how to generate squeezing, but there are limitations

Squeezed light is usually made with an optical parametric oscillator (OPO), a nonlinear crystal inside a cavity. An OPO gives squeezed light but no bright beam of its own: push it above threshold and the squeezing collapses. Bright squeezed beams are therefore made by adding a separate laser and locking the two together.

Sketch of a degenerate OPO; the pump power sweeps by itself until you drag the slider. Below threshold, the noise of the light inside the cavity follows the textbook result \(\Delta X^2=1/(1+\sqrt{P/P_\text{th}})\): at most 3 dB below vacuum, reached only at threshold. Above threshold the field becomes bright, picking one of two opposite phases, and the squeezing in \(X\) is lost (schematic).

The OPO also has practical demands. Its heart is a nonlinear crystal that splits pump photons at frequency \(2\omega\) into pairs of photons at \(\omega\). The effect is weak, so the crystal needs a strong pump and has to sit inside a cavity. The crystal is small, about a centimetre long, but it must be phase-matched: the pump and the light it creates have to travel through the crystal in step. If they drift apart, the light made further along cancels the light made earlier, and the OPO never turns on. Keeping them in step takes a carefully engineered crystal, held at a precisely controlled temperature.

The slider is the crystal's temperature (it sweeps by itself until you drag it). Temperature changes how fast light travels in the crystal, by a different amount for the pump and for the light at \(\omega\) (right). Only where the two speeds are equal do the waves stay in step: the light at \(\omega\) keeps growing and the OPO can turn on. Away from it, the light grows and fades again, and almost nothing comes out. Numbers for a 1 cm crystal of magnesium-doped lithium niobate turning 532 nm pump light into 1064 nm light, where temperature alone does the matching, at about 107 °C as measured for this crystal; the slopes of the speed curves are approximate. Squeezing experiments today mostly use periodically poled crystals, where the poling makes up the remaining speed difference, but they are tuned with temperature in the same way.

Another limitation of OPOs is that single-photon emission caps the intracavity squeezing at 3 dB, half the vacuum noise.

The promise of something different

A squeezed laser would give squeezing and a bright, coherent beam from one device. The idea has been proposed before (Navarrete-Benlloch, García-Ripoll and Porras 2014; Sánchez Muñoz and Jaksch 2021), but optical versions still needed squeezed light injected from outside. Part 2

A squeezed laser with trapped atoms

In the new scheme that we propose, the atoms do everything. No injection of squeezing from the outside, no fundamental limit to intracavity squeezing, no need of a nonlinear crystal, and coherence created autonomously.

The setup is a standard cavity-QED experiment: cold \(^{87}\)Rb atoms trapped in an optical cavity. Two ground states of the atom, \(|g\rangle\) and \(|e\rangle\) (6.8 GHz apart), make a two-level atom. The cavity mode is the light we want to squeeze. Its operator \(\hat a^\dagger\) adds a photon and \(\hat a\) removes one.

The atoms sit in two clouds. Both clouds are lit from the side by the same two Raman lasers, \(\Omega_r\) and \(\Omega_s\). These two lasers are the heart of the scheme. The next four steps show how they make every atom interact with a squeezed version of the cavity mode.

Sketch of the setup. The cooling and gain clouds play different roles in Part 3.

Inside one rubidium atom

Zoom into one atom. In \(^{87}\)Rb the ground state \(5^2S_{1/2}\) is split into two hyperfine levels, \(F=1\) and \(F=2\), 6.8 GHz apart. The excited state of the D1 line, \(5^2P_{1/2}\) (795 nm), is split into \(F'=1\) and \(F'=2\), 0.8 GHz apart. A weak magnetic field separates each level into Zeeman sublevels \(m_F\).

The scheme uses four of them. \(|g\rangle=|F{=}1,m_F{=}{-1}\rangle\) and \(|e\rangle=|F{=}2,m_F{=}{-2}\rangle\) form the two-level atom. \(|r\rangle\) and \(|s\rangle\), in \(F'=1\) and \(F'=2\), are the excited states that the Raman lasers pass near. The polarizations of the lasers and the cavity keep the atom inside this set.

Path 1: drop and emit

The laser \(\Omega_r\) is tuned so that an atom in \(|e\rangle\) can absorb a laser photon and emit a cavity photon, ending in \(|g\rangle\). The excited state \(|r\rangle\) is never really occupied: the laser misses it by about 2 GHz, so the atom only passes through a virtual level. This is a Raman transition.

In this process, the atom loses its energy and the cavity gains a photon: \(\hat\sigma\hat a^\dagger\), with \(\hat\sigma=|g\rangle\langle e|\). This is a coherent, reversible process: \(\hat\sigma^\dagger\hat a\) happens too. This is the familiar exchange of energy between an atom and light.

Path 2: rise and emit

The second laser, \(\Omega_s\), opens a stranger path. An atom in \(|g\rangle\) absorbs a laser photon, passes near \(|s\rangle\) and emits a cavity photon, but it ends in \(|e\rangle\). The atom is excited and a photon is created. The laser photon pays for both.

Without the laser, this process, \(\hat\sigma^\dagger\hat a^\dagger\), would not conserve energy and would be negligible. Here the laser makes it resonant, and the power of \(\Omega_s\) sets its strength. Its reverse, \(\hat\sigma\hat a\), takes the atom down while a photon disappears.

Both paths at once

With both lasers on, once the atom decays (\(\hat\sigma\)), the cavity simultaneously gains a photon (path 1, strength \(\lambda_r\)) and loses one (path 2, strength \(\lambda_{cr}\)):

\[\hat H=\hat\sigma\,\big(\lambda_r\,\hat a^\dagger+\lambda_{cr}\,\hat a\big)+\text{h.c.}\]

This strange exchange means the atom does not trade photons with \(\hat a\), but something else, a fixed blend of \(\hat a^\dagger\) and \(\hat a\). Writing \(\lambda_{cr}/\lambda_r=\tanh r\), the blend is \(g_0\,\hat a_s^\dagger\), where

\[\hat a_s=\hat a\cosh r+\hat a^\dagger\sinh r .\]

The ratio of the two laser amplitudes, \(\Omega_s/2\Omega_r=\tanh r\), sets \(r\). Drag it. (The figure shows the atom dropping; when it rises, the same happens in reverse.)

This \(\hat a_s\) is a well-known object, the Bogoliubov transformation of \(\hat a\). The transformation is unitary, and it connects the basis states (photon-number states, AKA Fock states) to a different class of states, squeezed Fock states. These states are copies of the original basis states, but their quadratures (coordinate axes in phase space) are rescaled copies of the real ones: \(X_s=e^{r}X\), \(P_s=e^{-r}P\), thereby squeezing fluctuations along the \(X\) axis, and enlarging them along \(P\) (here the axes are chosen arbitrarily; they are fixed by our Raman laser phases). Because \(\cosh^2 r-\sinh^2 r=1\), \(\hat a_s\) is still a perfectly good photon mode, with its own vacuum and coherent states. The vacuum of \(\hat a_s\) is a squeezed vacuum of the real light, and a coherent state of \(\hat a_s\) is a bright squeezed state. Anything ordinary the atoms do to \(\hat a_s\) turns into squeezing.

Part 3

Cooling and lasing in the squeezed basis

We now have an ordinary atom–light interaction in which the cavity mode is replaced by \(\hat a_s\). The rest of the scheme borrows two standard recipes from quantum optics: drain a mode of its energy and it will relax to its vacuum; pump atoms that emit into that same mode, and at some critical pumping, it will lase by stimulated emission. Applied to \(\hat a_s\), the first gives squeezed vacuum and the second gives squeezed laser light.

Squeezed cooling: a squeezed buffer

Start with the cooling cloud alone. Its atoms take excitations from \(\hat a_s\). A third laser, \(\Omega_b\), lets them hand those excitations on to a second cavity resonance of the mirrors, \(\hat b\), that leaks out very fast. Excitations only go one way: out. Together, the cooling atoms and \(\hat b\) form a squeezed buffer, a cold bath that only sees \(\hat a_s\). It drains \(\hat a_s\) at a rate \(\tilde\kappa\) to its vacuum, which is a squeezed vacuum of the real light.

This implementation leads to a basic question: how many cooling atoms does it take for it to work? The rate \(\tilde\kappa\) grows with the number of cooling atoms, \(N_c\). It competes with the cavity's ordinary loss through its mirror, \(\kappa\). That loss removes single photons of \(\hat a\), not of \(\hat a_s\), and lets plain vacuum noise back in. With too few cooling atoms the mirror wins; the buffer needs \(\tilde\kappa/\kappa\gg e^{2r}-1\). Too many atoms cause a different problem. The scheme relies on the excited states \(|r\rangle\) and \(|s\rangle\) staying virtual, far detuned from the lasers. But the atoms couple to the cavity collectively, with strength \(g_r\sqrt N\). Once that is no longer small next to the 2 GHz detuning, the excited levels start to fill, the atoms emit ordinary photons in random directions, and the mechanism breaks down. For the paper's parameters this sets \(N\ll10^5\). In the sweet spot in between, the paper finds up to about 9 dB of squeezing inside the cavity, well past the 3 dB of an OPO.

Squeezed cooling, live

Drag the number of cooling atoms \(N_c\), or let it sweep. As the cloud grows, the buffer drains \(\hat a_s\) faster and more photons leave through \(\hat b\). The Wigner function of the cavity, drawn inside it, shrinks from a round vacuum cloud into a squeezed ellipse.

Push \(N_c\) too far and the excited level \(|r\rangle\) starts to fill: the atoms shoot out ordinary photons in random directions (red), and the model no longer holds.

Pumping the gain atoms

Now pump the second cloud, the gain atoms, into \(|e\rangle\) with weak repumping lasers.

Two repumpers do the job, both on the D1 line: one drives \(F=1\to F'=2\), the other \(F=2\to F'=1\). After each absorption the atom falls back by spontaneous emission to a random ground sublevel, and the cycle repeats. The beams are polarized so that they contain only \(\pi\) and \(\sigma^-\) light, which can keep \(m_F\) or lower it, never raise it. The only ground sublevel that neither repumper can excite is \(|e\rangle=|F{=}2,m_F{=}{-2}\rangle\): there is no excited level for it to go to. It is a dark state, and the population piles up there.

This repumping scheme has to be designed carefully, with the correct polarizations, so that the repumpers take the atom to \(|e\rangle\) even when it has left the two Zeeman sublevels that we chose as our two-level system. The repumpers are also kept weak, so the excited states stay nearly empty. Seen from the two-level atom, the net effect is an incoherent pump from \(|g\rangle\) to \(|e\rangle\).

Cooling plus gain: lasing

Through the same Raman coupling, these gain atoms couple to \(\hat a_s\) and experience stimulated emission. They see the squeezed buffer as just the cavity's loss. Once the gain beats that loss, \(\hat a_s\) lases.

A laser settles into a coherent state of its own mode. Here that mode is \(\hat a_s\), so the real light is a bright displaced squeezed state. It is a beam with its own carrier, a laser-like coherence time and noise below vacuum in \(X\), and it leaves through the cavity mirror.

A slowly drifting carrier

The cavity now holds a bright squeezed state. Like any laser, its phase is not fixed: it drifts slowly and randomly, so over long times the carrier wanders along a ring. The squeezed axis, set by the Raman lasers, does not move.

The drift is slow, because the laser line is much narrower than the cavity's decay rates. Over any time shorter than the coherence time \(1/\Delta\nu\), where \(\Delta\nu\) is the linewidth, the carrier is effectively frozen relative to the ellipse, and the field keeps its quiet crests.

The spectrum of squeezing

A homodyne detector reads the noise in \(X\) at each sideband frequency \(\nu\) around the reference set by the Raman lasers; 0 dB is the vacuum. In the lasing phase, two kinds of features appear.

At \(\nu\approx0\) sits a narrow peak: the laser's own carrier. This is atom-like, because the laser has a superradiant component: a big part of the coherence is stored in the atoms, not the light, and that locks the laser at a frequency set by the atoms (making it robust to cavity fluctuations). Here, that atomic frequency is, in practice, set by the average of the Raman drives.

This lasing peak carries excess noise, which comes from the slow phase drift. It is narrow because the drift is exceptionally slow, a hallmark of lasing. To each side, cavity-like sidebands about \(\kappa+\tilde\kappa\) wide dip below the vacuum. That is the squeezing, locked to the carrier.

In short

  • Two Raman paths, one squeezed mode. One laser makes the atom emit while it drops, the other while it rises. Together they couple the atom to the Bogoliubov mode \(\hat a_s\), and the ratio of laser amplitudes sets \(r\).
  • Squeezed cooling. A cooling cloud plus a lossy mode act as a squeezed buffer that drains \(\hat a_s\) to its vacuum: up to about 9 dB of squeezing inside the cavity.
  • Squeezed lasing. A pumped gain cloud lases into \(\hat a_s\). The result is bright, coherent light with noise below vacuum, with no crystal and no injected squeezed light.

In numbers. With realistic cold-atom parameters, the sweet spot is about \(10^3\) or more cooling atoms and \(10^2\)–\(10^3\) gain atoms. Tuned to squeeze its output beam rather than the light inside the cavity, the device is predicted to reach up to about 18 dB below the lasing threshold. Above threshold, the laser beam has noise below vacuum over a megahertz-wide band of sideband frequencies, away from the narrow carrier peak.

Also in the paper: the full atomic level scheme and the laser frequencies that cancel unwanted terms; the repumper design; lasing thresholds, superradiant emission and linewidth narrowing; the phase diagram in the two atom numbers, including a chaotic regime; the noise spectrum of the beam; the trade-off between squeezing inside the cavity and in the output; and when strong lasing starts to excite the cooling atoms.
